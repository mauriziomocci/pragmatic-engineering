# documentation-quality

This skill says how documentation is divided and what it may claim. How code is commented, how a document is reviewed and how it is scored live elsewhere, and the last section points to them.

## When to use

Load it before writing, splitting or reorganising documentation: a README, a guide, a reference page, an explanation, a manual. Do not load it for a docstring or a comment, whose rules are in `engineering-principles`, nor for a message to a person, which follows the user's writing rules.

## Four kinds of documentation

The division comes from Diataxis, by Daniele Procida; https://diataxis.fr is recommended reading, and nothing here is copied from it. A page serves one reader in one situation, and four situations cover almost all of them:

- a **tutorial** takes a newcomer through a first success, step by step, where every step works and the reader learns by doing;
- a **how-to guide** gives someone who already knows the product the steps to reach one goal of their own;
- a **reference** describes the machinery as it is (commands, options, fields, defaults), complete and dry, to be looked up;
- an **explanation** says why the thing is built the way it is, the alternatives and the trade-offs, to be read away from the keyboard.

Two questions tell them apart. Does the page guide what the reader does, or what the reader knows? Is the reader learning, or working? Doing and learning is a tutorial; doing and working, a how-to guide; knowing and working, a reference; knowing and learning, an explanation. A page that answers both ways mixes two kinds and is split: one kind per page, with links between them.

One exception is declared, the entry page. A README lines up, for someone arriving, the first session, the guides, the explanation and the reference, each in a section of its own and of one kind only.

## Only what exists

A document describes the system as it is today. A command, a flag, a path or a feature that does not exist yet is not described as present: it is left out, or labelled as planned, with no instructions to follow. Every page that describes a control says what the control does not cover, because a page that says only what works is advertising. Counts, lists and examples are checked against the source in the same change that touches them, and a claim is checked against the code, never against the previous version of the document. A rule that only a document states, with nothing that enforces it, is named as such.

## The manual for daily users

A project whose interface people use every day keeps a manual for them: for the people who run the product day after day, not for the system administrators. The tools of the platform (error pages, developer tools, data loaders, the administration of the framework) stay out of it. It holds a tutorial for the first journey and a how-to guide for each task.

- A spec that touches such a page lists the chapters of the manual it affects, and the work is not done until those chapters say what the page now does.
- Screenshots are generated by a script from a preview with demonstration data, never taken by hand, and all of them are made again with one command.
- The text lives once, in Markdown. The other formats (a PDF, a word-processor document, slides) are generated from it by a command, and no format is kept up to date by hand.

## Where the other rules live

- Documentation changes in the same commit as the code, docstrings say why, identifiers are in English, and the answer to a technical question has its shape: `engineering-principles`.
- The review of a document, with its documentation lens (claims against the code, drifted counts, the missing limit, commands nobody ran, kinds mixed on one page): `blunt-review`.
- The score of the documentation lens and its thresholds: `quality-scoring`.
